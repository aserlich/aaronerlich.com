I counted every referee report I have written

publishing
peer review
transparency
data viz
Ten years of peer review, logged report by report: 126 reports for 63 journals, 71,603 words. Whether that leaves me in credit or in debt turns out to depend entirely on an assumption nobody states — and the journals that tell me least are the ones I write least for.
Author

Aaron Erlich

Published

October 21, 2026

A while ago I put my whole publication record online, and then asked whether it mattered where the papers landed. Both were about what I have received from peer review. This one is about what I have given.

I went back through every portal, decision letter and folder I could find and logged each report individually: which journal, which round, how many words, what I recommended, and what the journal told me afterwards. 126 reports, 63 journals, 95 manuscripts, 71,603 words, 2015 to 2026. It is a census rather than an estimate, which turns out to matter — my own prior guess was “about 120 to 130 reviews for maybe 65 journals”, and I was close on the first and wrong about the shape.

This post is the record and how it was built. The arguments it suggests are for later posts.

2026 is hollow and dashed because the year is not over. The flat line is the benchmark: Djupe and Walker's 2024 survey of 637 political scientists finds that faculty in PhD-granting programmes average about six reviews a year, and about 5.8 for associate professors like me. My mean over 2021–25 is 14.4, and 2025 was 29.

So: roughly two and a half times the disciplinary average, and 2025 alone lands in the far-right “20+” bin of their distribution — the tail they describe as shrinking. I am in the group that does roughly half of all reviewing while being about a fifth of the people.

One honest deflation. Their question asks you to “check your records to be accurate”, and almost nobody has records. Re-reviews of a paper you saw before are exactly what recall loses first. I have records, so my number is more complete than theirs — some of that 2.4× gap is measurement rather than labour. Counting only first-round reports I am still at 10.8 a year, about 1.8×, which is the floor.

Sorted by scope, not prestige. "Outside political science" means the journal belongs to another discipline — psychology, communication, economics, public policy — rather than being genuinely cross-field.

Only 17% of my reports are for the top three. The bulk sits in subfield journals and, strikingly, outside political science: interdisciplinary and non-PS venues together are 46 of 126 reports.

The long tail is the real shape. 35 of the 63 journals have exactly one report from me; 48 have one or two. These are not journals I have a relationship with — they are journals that found me for a specialism, mostly post-Soviet politics, corruption, survey methods or disinformation. Most of them I have never submitted to, which in ledger terms is pure giving.

And it has been moving. In 2015–21, 27% of my reports were for journals outside core political science. In 2022–26 it is 52%, and in 2025 it was 69% — my busiest year was also my least disciplinary one. That matters for everything below, because those are the venues that tell me least and that I write the shortest reports for.

Median length by round: 656 → 216 → 98 → 78 words. 73% of my reports are first reviews. The darker bar is the report proper; the gap above it is the reference list, which is small because most reports have none — see below.

This matters more than it looks, because every survey of reviewing asks for one number. Djupe and Walker’s Q17 is “how many article reviews have you conducted… in the past year?”, with nothing said about whether a re-review of a paper you already saw counts. Someone who did ten first reviews and someone who did five firsts plus five re-reviews both answer “ten” and have done very different amounts of work.

I would add one field:

How many article reviews have you conducted…? Count every report you submitted, including repeat reviews of a revised version of a paper you had reviewed before.

Of those, how many were first reviews of a manuscript that was new to you?

As for what I say: of 84 first-round recommendations with a real term, 43 were reject and 41 were major revisions. Not one was accept or minor. I have never once opened with a positive recommendation. I did not know that about myself.

Who gets a reading list

I had the parser separate the reference list from the report proper, which turned out to be worth doing. Only 31 of my 123 counted reports cite anything at all, and references are 2.7% of everything I have written as a referee. So “report length” and “report length excluding citations” are almost the same number — but which reports carry a bibliography is not random.

First reports only. Small cells — the top-3 row is 1 of 14. Citing is almost entirely a first-round activity: 32% of first reports, 5% of second ones.

It runs opposite to length. I write the longest reports for the top three and almost never hand them a reading list (1 of 14). I write the shortest reports for journals furthest from political science and hand those a reading list about half the time.

That is not inconsistent, I think. At a top-3 journal the authors already know the literature and what I can add is scrutiny. At an area-studies or interdisciplinary venue I am often there precisely because I know a political science literature the paper has not met — so the useful thing I can do is name it. Looking through those reports, a striking number supply the same kind of thing: this mechanism already has a name in my field, here are three papers.

Citing is also almost entirely a first-round act — 32% of first reports against 5% of second ones. By round two the argument about what the paper should have read is over.

The part I cannot settle

Here is the question the whole exercise was for: have I done my share? I have 126 reports given. Against that, my own submissions consumed referee labour — 86 attempts at journals, which used 163 referees that I know of.

That sounds like a clean subtraction. It is not.

      textAnchor: d => d.balance > 0 ? "start" : "end", dx: d => d.balance > 0 ? 6 : -6 })
  ]

The same record, five ways. Dark bar is the accounting I think is defensible: first-round reports on both sides, with the obligation divided among coauthors.

The spread is −132 to +57 on one unchanged record, and every step is arguable:

  • Do you charge a flat three reviews per submission? That norm comes from a blog post by Ben Lauderdale, not from any study, and when Djupe and Walker actually asked, agreement was lukewarm. It also charges me for desk rejects: 8 of my 13 consumed no referee at all. My submissions have really cost 2.47 reviews each, not three.
  • Do you divide by coauthors? A report consumed by a four-author paper is not one report I owe. Dividing flips the sign. But it should probably only count coauthors who are themselves in the reviewer pool, and graduate students largely are not — which I cannot compute, so I have not.
  • Do you compare like with like? My giving side counts re-reviews; the consuming side counts distinct referees. Only first-round-against-first-round is honest.

So the defensible number is +23, a modest surplus — and I want to be clear that I chose the assumptions that produce it, which is exactly the problem. “Am I pulling my weight” has no agreed denominator. I think that is the most useful thing in this post.

One known bound, stated rather than hidden: 36 of my submissions went through at least one round of revisions, so roughly 43 later rounds of refereeing happened on my behalf that my ledger does not count. At two referees each that is ~86 further reports — enough to reverse the sign. Collecting them is the obvious next job.

Caveat that belongs here and not in a footnote: "did not go back" conflates I declined with they never asked again, and my invitation log is too thin to separate them. This also only covers journals I said yes to once, so it shows where I did not return, not where I never went.

I have a rule I only half-knew I had: if a journal never shows me the other referees’ reports and never tells me the outcome, I do not review for it again. The log says I keep it. I went back to 39% of the journals that share the reports and 5% of the ones that do not — one in twenty against two in five.

Journals where the practice is established; cells are small, and the top-3 row rests on a single journal. The ordering survived me rebuilding the classification three times.

The gradient is not prestige. It is distance from my own discipline. Political science journals return the other reports; area-studies, interdisciplinary and non-PS journals mostly do not. Which has an uncomfortable corollary for anyone whose work crosses fields: the further your expertise takes you from your home discipline, the worse the terms you referee on. 46 of my 126 reports are for exactly those venues.

I rebuilt the journal classification three times while writing this — moving regulation and governance into political science, psychology and communication out of it — each time on disciplinary grounds without looking at what it would do. The ordering came out the same every time.

Put the three gradients together and they all run along the same axis. As a journal sits further from political science, it is less likely to show me the other reports, I write it a shorter report, and I am more likely to hand it a reading list. And that is the part of my reviewing that has been growing.

How this was built, and what it cost

The data is a local R project; what reaches this page is an aggregate file with no manuscript titles, no ids, no dates finer than a year, and nothing at all from my co-referees’ reports. That separation is enforced by a test rather than by my memory of it.

Three things I learned building it that I would tell anyone attempting the same:

A blank cell is not one thing. I started with empty cells and ended up needing four codes: not applicable (I cannot know when a co-referee was invited), unknown, recoverable but not yet fetched, and withheld — the journal had it and would not show me. That last one stops being bookkeeping the moment you try to count how often you were refused: three of my reports sit at AJPS behind a page that returns “You have arrived at this page in an unauthorized manner”, because I joined that manuscript at a later round and the earlier reports are closed to me.

The rating forms do not agree with each other. Thirteen different response scales across these journals, and three of them run backwards — on one, a 1 is the best score; on another, a 5 is. Two scales change meaning by question: on the same Yes/No form, “Overall Quality: No” is a bad review and “Financial Competing Interest: No” is a clean declaration.

Your own labour is not reliably retrievable. SAGE Open served me a scoresheet from 2019 when I asked this month. One journal’s system does not store the review at all, and another does not list it as having happened. If you want this record, the time to keep it is now.

What this cannot answer

It only contains journals I said yes to, so it can tell me where not to go back, not where not to go. It is thinnest in the early years, where the record is worst. And the consuming side is incomplete in the specific way described above.

What I would most like is for this to be unremarkable — for enough people to post their own that the denominator question gets settled by something other than one person’s assumptions. If you keep this kind of record, or want to, I would like to hear from you.
